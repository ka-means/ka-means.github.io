01 Decision-Making Under Uncertainty

How should an intelligent system act when it cannot know the world completely?

01 Decision-Making Under Uncertainty

Master Question: How should an intelligent system act when it cannot know the world completely?

What We Want to Discover

Real autonomous agents rarely observe the true state of the world directly.

A robot sees through cameras, lidar, radar, proprioception and imperfect localisation. A satellite observes only what its sensors can capture during limited observation windows. A remote autonomous system may receive telemetry late or intermittently. A multi-agent system observes other agents through actions, messages and inferred intentions rather than through direct access to their internal states.

In all these cases there is a separation between the world and what the agent knows about it:

\[s_t \rightarrow o_t \rightarrow a_t\]

where $s_t$ is the underlying state of the world, $o_t$ is the information available to the agent, and $a_t$ is the action selected by the agent.

The observation may be incomplete, noisy, missing, delayed or ambiguous. The agent may encounter situations its internal model does not represent. Yet it must still decide.

This area investigates how an intelligent system should represent what it does not know, how that representation should change as evidence arrives, how uncertainty should influence action, when additional information should be acquired, how uncertainty should interact with risk, and when an autonomous system should recognise that it should not act.

The deeper question is therefore not simply how an agent can act under uncertainty. It is: what information is actually necessary for intelligent action?

Why It Matters

Almost every other area in this portfolio quietly assumes some solution to this problem.

A reinforcement-learning agent assumes its inputs contain enough information to learn a useful policy. A robot assumes perception provides a sufficiently useful representation of its surroundings. A multi-agent system reasons from incomplete information about other agents. An explainable agent may need to explain not only why it acted, but what it believed when it acted. A controllable autonomous system must determine whether its uncertainty is sufficiently high to request human intervention.

If the information problem is handled incorrectly, errors can propagate:

\[\text{incorrect observation} \rightarrow \text{incorrect representation} \rightarrow \text{incorrect decision} \rightarrow \text{incorrect action}\]

But the relationship is subtler than this chain suggests. An agent can possess an inaccurate representation of the world and still choose the correct action. Conversely, it can possess a highly accurate representation while missing one variable that completely changes what it should do.

This suggests that intelligent action may depend less on knowing everything than on knowing what matters for the decision.

Framing the Problem

State Is Not Observation

The foundational distinction is:

\[s_t \neq o_t\]

The state represents the underlying configuration of the system relevant to its future evolution. The observation represents information available to the agent about that state.

In a fully observable environment, $o_t = s_t$. But physical agents operate through a stochastic observation process:

\[o_t \sim O(o_t \mid s_t)\]

A more realistic architecture is therefore:

\[s_t \rightarrow o_t \rightarrow b_t \rightarrow a_t\]

where $b_t$ is an internal representation of what the agent currently believes about the world, distinct from $o_t$, which is what it received. The nature of $b_t$, whether a history of observations, a point estimate, a probability distribution over states, a learned latent vector, or a world-model state, is itself one of the central questions this area investigates.

Forms of Imperfect Information

Partial observability is not one homogeneous phenomenon. Each form presents a structurally different problem.

Partial observation. Some relevant part of the state is hidden: $o_t \sim O(o_t \mid s_t)$ assigns zero probability to certain components of $s_t$.

Noisy observation. The relevant quantity is measured imperfectly. For continuous states: $o_t = s_t + \epsilon_t$. The signal is present but corrupted.

Missing observation. Information normally available is temporarily absent: $o_t = \varnothing$. The agent must act without current evidence.

Delayed observation. The information may be accurate but describe an earlier state: $o_t = s_{t-d}$. The agent may receive perfectly correct information about a world that no longer exists.

Ambiguous observation. Different hidden states produce indistinguishable observations: $s_i \neq s_j$ while $O(s_i) = O(s_j)$. The sensor may be functioning perfectly. The observation itself is insufficient to distinguish the states.

Novel state. The system encounters a situation outside the states, concepts or distributions represented by its previous learning. The question changes from which known state am I in? to does my representation contain the situation I am experiencing at all?

The Central Distinctions

Two propositions organise the experimental programme of this area.

State uncertainty is not decision uncertainty

Suppose $P(s_1) = P(s_2) = 0.5$, but $a^(s_1) = a^(s_2) = a_1$. The state is uncertain. The decision is not.

Now suppose $a^(s_1) = a_1$ and $a^(s_2) = a_2$. A small change in belief may now change the preferred action.

\[\boxed{\text{state uncertainty} \neq \text{decision uncertainty}}\]

Information gain is not decision value

An observation could greatly reduce entropy while leaving the preferred action unchanged. Another observation could reduce uncertainty only slightly but reveal the one variable that changes the decision.

\[\boxed{\text{information gain} \neq \text{decision value}}\]

These are not merely conceptual claims. Both will be tested empirically across the F-series, M-series and research projects.

Foundations & Pioneers

The problem of decision-making under uncertainty did not emerge from a single discipline.

Probability theory asked how uncertain beliefs should be represented. Statistics asked how evidence should change decisions. Economics asked how choices should be made when information is incomplete or costly. Control theory asked how a hidden dynamic system could be estimated and regulated. Cybernetics asked how much information and behavioural variety a regulator requires. Information theory asked how uncertainty could be quantified and transmitted. Psychology asked how organisms infer useful properties of the world from uncertain cues. Game theory asked how decisions change when uncertainty concerns other agents. Complex-systems research asked how adaptive behaviour can emerge when no agent possesses a complete description of the system.

Area 01 therefore inherits not one intellectual lineage, but several. The purpose of this section is not to claim that these traditions were all solving the same problem. They were not. The question we ask instead is: what version of the uncertainty problem did each tradition discover?

Not every figure below generates a direct F-series experiment. The F-series covers mechanisms most directly transferable to an autonomous agent in simulation. The broader cast of founders prevents Area 01 from being reduced to a single formal tradition and keeps the two central distinctions of this area visible from multiple angles.

1. Probability as Belief

Before an agent can act under uncertainty, it needs a language for uncertainty itself. The probability-as-belief tradition builds that language. Its contribution is not an algorithm but a foundation: uncertainty can be represented explicitly, revised as evidence arrives, and connected to action through preference. Every subsequent tradition assumes this foundation is in place. Without it, there is no belief state to update, no prior to revise, and no basis for asking whether a given observation was worth obtaining.

Thomas Bayes. How should uncertainty about an unknown cause change when evidence arrives?

The Bayesian tradition introduces the basic transformation: prior + evidence to posterior. For Area 01, the important contribution is not a particular formula but the possibility that an agent can represent uncertainty explicitly and revise that representation as observations arrive. This creates the intellectual foundation for the belief state $b_t(s) = P(s_t = s \mid h_t)$, although the modern formalism would emerge much later.

Frank Ramsey. Can uncertainty be represented as degrees of belief revealed through choices?

Ramsey’s Truth and Probability (1926, published posthumously) developed an account of partial belief and subjective probability. Rather than treating probability only as an objective frequency, probabilities could represent degrees of belief that influence rational action. This begins connecting belief with action rather than treating probability purely as a property of external events.

Bruno de Finetti. Is uncertainty a property of the world, a property of the agent’s knowledge, or both?

De Finetti treated probability as an expression of an individual’s uncertainty rather than something existing independently in the external world. This question becomes important when separating environmental stochasticity, observation uncertainty, model uncertainty and epistemic uncertainty, distinctions that Area 01 will need throughout.

Leonard Savage. How should an agent act given uncertain beliefs?

Savage’s The Foundations of Statistics (1954) formalised a personalist theory of decision involving subjective probabilities, preferences and utility: belief + utility to choice. Savage bridges the probability and decision traditions and appears in each for different contributions. Here his importance is the transition $b_t \rightarrow a_t$, not only how beliefs are formed, but how they produce action.

2. Statistical Decision Theory

A language for uncertainty is not enough. The statistical decision tradition asks the harder question: what should you do with it? The key insight is that information cannot be evaluated independently of the decision problem. A perfect measurement of the wrong variable is useless. An imperfect measurement of the right variable may be decisive. This tradition is the historical root of both central distinctions of Area 01, state uncertainty is not decision uncertainty, and information gain is not decision value, and it establishes those distinctions long before reinforcement learning made them computationally tractable.

Abraham Wald. Which decision rule minimises expected loss across all possible states?

Wald’s Statistical Decision Functions (1950) established a framework connecting unknown states, observations, actions, loss functions and decision rules. The key insight for Area 01: information is relevant because different actions have different consequences under different states. Uncertainty cannot be evaluated independently of the decision problem.

David Blackwell. When does one information structure actually produce better decisions than another?

Blackwell’s 1951 work on the comparison of experiments asked when one source of information enables strictly better decisions than another. This is particularly important for this portfolio because it shifts attention from how much information does an observation contain? to can this information structure produce better decisions? Two sensors can be compared not merely by their noise levels but by the decisions they enable. This provides the historical precursor to one of the central distinctions of the area:

\[\boxed{\text{information gain} \neq \text{decision value}}\]

Howard Raiffa and Robert Schlaifer. How much should an agent be willing to pay for another observation?

Applied Statistical Decision Theory (1961) systematically connected subjective probability, sampling, utility and decision analysis. Their treatment explicitly includes expected value of perfect information and expected value of sample information. The problem becomes: value of observation equals expected improvement in decision minus cost of observation. This is a direct ancestor of Project 01.4, Active Sensing.

3. State, Dynamics and Sequential Decisions

The belief and decision traditions deal, at their core, with single-stage problems. The sequential tradition introduces time. A decision now changes what states are reachable later and what observations will be available next. This reframes uncertainty from a static estimation problem into an ongoing relationship between action, state and information. Markov tells us what information from the past needs to survive into the present. Bellman tells us how to account for futures that have not happened yet. Howard reveals the assumption that the entire experimental programme of Area 01 will challenge: that the relevant state is available to the decision maker.

Andrey Markov. What information about the past is sufficient to predict the future?

\[P(s_{t+1} \mid s_t, s_{t-1}, \ldots) = P(s_{t+1} \mid s_t)\]

The key question: what information from the past must the present state retain? This question will reappear almost unchanged in Project 01.2: how much memory is sufficient?

Richard Bellman. How should present decisions account for future consequences?

\[V(s) = \max_a \left[ R(s,a) + \gamma \mathbb{E}[V(s')] \right]\]

Dynamic programming transforms decision-making into a sequential problem. The question changes from what should I do given uncertainty now? to how should I act now when today’s decision changes tomorrow’s possibilities?

Ronald Howard. What if the decision maker cannot observe $s_t$?

Howard’s work on dynamic programming and Markov processes consolidated the MDP framework connecting state, transition, reward and policy in a sequential decision problem. In doing so, it makes the central assumption that Area 01 challenges: that the relevant state is available to the decision maker.

4. Feedback, Information and Hidden-State Control

This is where the uncertainty problem becomes physical and, at one point, epistemologically recursive. Control theory and cybernetics ask what happens when observation and action are coupled in a continuous loop, when what the agent does changes what it will observe next, and when the state it needs to act on cannot be directly seen. This tradition also contains the deepest rupture in the founding literature: the move from first-order cybernetics, where a system observes its environment, to second-order cybernetics, where a system observes itself observing. That rupture is Bateson’s, and it produces the most precise formulation of the central distinction of this area.

Norbert Wiener. How should observation and action interact through feedback?

Cybernetics connected control, communication and feedback. The central loop: observe, act, environment changes, observe again. What the agent does determines what world it will observe next. Observation and action are not independent.

Gregory Bateson. What if the observer is part of the system being observed, and information is only what makes a difference to behaviour?

Bateson’s second-order cybernetics introduces a move that Wiener’s framework does not make: the system that is observing is itself part of the system being observed. This produces a recursive loop that first-order control theory cannot represent. For Area 01, Bateson’s most direct contribution is his definition of information: a difference that makes a difference. This is the most precise formulation available of the distinction between information gain and decision value. A difference in the world that does not change what the agent does is not information in the relevant sense. Bateson also distinguished levels of learning, change in response, change in the set from which responses are selected, change in how learning itself occurs, that map directly onto the progression from 01.1 through 01.6. An agent that recognises its own representational limits (01.6) is operating at a level of learning that earlier projects do not require.

Claude Shannon. How can uncertainty be quantified, and when does information actually reduce it?

Shannon’s 1948 formalism provides the measure of uncertainty as entropy: $H(S) = -\sum_s P(s) \log P(s)$. This allows uncertainty to become measurable and supports concepts such as information gain, mutual information and sensor selection. But Area 01 will explicitly test a deeper question: does reducing entropy always improve decisions? The Blackwell result already suggests it does not. Shannon provides the formal tools; whether those tools map cleanly onto decision value is what the F-series will examine.

W. Ross Ashby. How much distinguishable variety must a controller possess to regulate the variety it encounters?

Ashby’s Law of Requisite Variety, from An Introduction to Cybernetics (1956), states that a regulator must possess at least as much variety as the disturbances it needs to suppress. For Area 01, this offers a different lens on observability. If many relevant environmental situations collapse into the same observation, then the controller may lack the informational variety necessary to respond differently: $s_i \neq s_j$ but $O(s_i) = O(s_j)$, which becomes problematic when $a^(s_i) \neq a^(s_j)$. Ashby’s perspective connects directly with decision-relevant observability.

Rudolf Kalman. How can a hidden dynamic state be estimated from noisy measurements?

Kalman’s 1960 filtering work provides a recursive solution: model prediction plus new measurement produces an updated estimate. The central move is that $o_t \neq s_t$, but the history $o_{1:t}, a_{1:t-1}$ may allow reconstruction of useful information about the hidden state. This directly motivates Area 01’s core comparison: $o_t$ vs $h_t$ vs $\hat{s}_t$ vs $b_t$.

5. Strategic Uncertainty and Other Agents

Every previous tradition treats the source of uncertainty as passive: a noisy sensor, a hidden physical state, a stochastic environment. Game theory introduces a qualitatively different problem. The hidden state may contain another agent who is also reasoning, deciding and potentially modelling you. The uncertainty is no longer merely about the world; it is about what another mind knows, believes and intends. Area 01 inherits this problem directly the moment any agent in the portfolio interacts with another, and the multi-agent areas of this portfolio will depend on the foundations laid here.

John von Neumann and Oskar Morgenstern. How should an agent act when the environment includes another entity that is also deciding?

Theory of Games and Economic Behavior (1944) established a formal framework for strategic decision problems involving multiple decision makers and explicitly considered imperfect information. This opens a qualitatively different uncertainty problem: the hidden state may contain another agent’s strategy, not merely a physical configuration.

John Harsanyi. What if uncertainty concerns the other agent’s type, information or preferences?

Harsanyi’s 1967 to 1968 work on games with incomplete information introduced a systematic Bayesian treatment in which players hold subjective probability distributions over parameters such as other players’ payoffs, strategies and information. The uncertainty problem expands from what state is the world in? to what does the other agent know? and eventually to what does the other agent believe that I know? This becomes crucial once the portfolio reaches multi-agent autonomy.

6. Bounded Rationality

Every optimal solution in the traditions above assumes an agent that can compute what needs to be computed. Simon breaks this assumption without discarding the rest of the framework. Even if an exact belief state exists mathematically, a real agent, biological or artificial, may not be able to represent it, update it, or act on it within the available time and memory. This introduces a second kind of sufficiency that the portfolio cannot ignore alongside informational sufficiency: computational sufficiency. An approximate representation that can be maintained and used may be strictly preferable to an exact one that cannot.

Herbert Simon. Even if an exact belief state exists mathematically, can a real agent represent and compute it?

In A Behavioral Model of Rational Choice (1955) and subsequent work, Simon developed an alternative to models assuming unrestricted rational optimisation. For Area 01, this introduces a second notion of sufficiency beyond the informational one: computational sufficiency. An approximate representation may be preferable if an exact one cannot be maintained or used in time. The question becomes not only what is the optimal representation? but what representation is computationally feasible?

7. Radical and Distributed Uncertainty

Every previous tradition assumes that the relevant states can at least be enumerated in principle, even when they cannot be directly observed or computed. Knight and Hayek challenge this assumption from two directions. Knight argues that some uncertainty cannot be reduced to known probability distributions because the space of possibilities is not fully specified in advance. Hayek argues that even if the state space were known in principle, the relevant knowledge may never exist in concentrated form in a single observer. Together, they sharpen Project 01.6 and the Autonomy Boundary phenomenon: an agent may need to recognise not only that it does not know the state, but that its model of what states are possible may itself be incomplete.

Frank Knight. What if uncertainty cannot be reduced to known probability distributions?

Knight’s Risk, Uncertainty and Profit (1921) drew a distinction between forms of uncertainty that can be treated probabilistically and situations where the relevant possibilities are not enumerable in advance. For Area 01, this raises a deeper problem. A POMDP typically assumes a defined state space $\mathcal{S}$. But what happens when the agent’s model does not contain the situation it encounters? Then the problem is not merely $P(s_i \mid h_t)$; it is is the true situation represented anywhere inside $\mathcal{S}$? This provides a conceptual bridge toward Project 01.6, Unknown Unknowns.

Friedrich Hayek. What if the relevant knowledge is never available in concentrated form to a single observer?

Hayek’s 1945 The Use of Knowledge in Society argues that economically relevant knowledge exists as dispersed, incomplete and sometimes contradictory knowledge held by different individuals, not as a global state available to any single mind. This differs from ordinary partial observability, where we imagine $s_t$ exists as a well-defined global state that the agent simply cannot see. The Hayekian problem is more radical: the complete informational object we call the state may never exist in concentrated form. For Area 01, the important transfer is the informational principle: effective action may sometimes require a sufficient local signal rather than reconstruction of a complete global state. This resonates directly with the distinction between world state and decision-relevant information.

8. Probabilistic Perception

Just before modern reinforcement learning, experimental psychology arrived independently at the same architecture: distal world property to uncertain cues to judgment to action. Brunswik and Gibson provide something the formal traditions do not: empirical evidence about how biological systems actually handle the gap between world and observation. The formal traditions tell us what an optimal agent should do; this tradition tells us what evolved and learned systems actually do, which is not always the same thing. Their findings about cue integration, ecological validity and affordances become direct inputs to the design of the M-series experiments and to the critical-variables and noise experiments in 01.1.

Egon Brunswik. Can an agent integrate imperfect cues to recover decision-relevant properties of a hidden world?

Brunswik argued for a probabilistic view of organisms operating in environments where cues are imperfectly related to distal properties of the world. His 1955 work explicitly described functional psychology as probabilistic. The structure is remarkably close to our problem: distal world property to uncertain cues to judgment to action. An organism rarely observes the property it cares about directly. Instead, it combines cues whose ecological validity is imperfect. This gives us a psychological formulation of $s_t \rightarrow o_t \rightarrow a_t$ long before modern reinforcement learning, and is especially relevant to experiments involving noisy observations, redundant cues, conflicting cues, critical variables and cue reliability.

James J. Gibson. Does an agent need to reconstruct the world, or only perceive what the world affords it to do?

Gibson’s ecological approach, developed in The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception (1979), emphasised information available in organism-environment interaction and the perception of possibilities for action, affordances. Rather than assuming that perception always requires reconstructing a complete internal model of the external world, Gibson pointed toward a direct relationship between available information and actionable structure. This is another route toward decision-relevant representation: not reconstruction, but sufficient coupling.

9. Adaptive Systems

The previous traditions treat uncertainty as epistemic: the agent cannot see the state, cannot compute the belief, cannot afford the observation, or cannot enumerate the possibilities. This tradition introduces a different kind of problem. What happens when the system the agent is trying to navigate is itself changing in non-linear, potentially irreversible ways? Bertalanffy shows that open systems are constitutively different from closed ones. Prigogine shows that systems far from equilibrium can produce uncertainty that is intrinsic to the dynamics rather than merely to the observer, uncertainty that more information cannot resolve, because the future trajectory is genuinely undetermined at a bifurcation point. Holland shows that useful representations can emerge through adaptation rather than being specified in advance. Together, these three figures shift the question from how does the agent handle an uncertain world? to what happens when the world’s uncertainty is structural, not incidental?

Ludwig von Bertalanffy. Can the state relevant to behaviour belong to an interacting system rather than an isolated agent?

General System Theory distinguished closed systems from open systems that exchange matter and influence with their environments. For Area 01, its contribution is primarily conceptual: the relevant state may not be localised in a single agent but may emerge from system interaction. This becomes increasingly important when autonomy is embedded in ecosystems, organisations or multi-agent systems.

Ilya Prigogine. What if the uncertainty is not in the observer but in the dynamics of the system itself?

The other founders treat uncertainty as a problem of knowledge: the agent does not have access to a state that nevertheless exists in a determinate form. Prigogine’s work on dissipative structures and systems far from equilibrium introduces a different kind of problem. In non-linear systems operating far from equilibrium, small perturbations near bifurcation points can produce radically different trajectories, and this indeterminacy is not resolved by obtaining more information. The state of the system after a bifurcation is not hidden; it is genuinely undetermined in advance. For Area 01, this matters in three places: it provides theoretical grounding for irrecoverable uncertainty that belongs to the system rather than to the observer; it is the historical precursor to information thresholds and the idea of a critical point $\theta_c$ after which behaviour collapses; and it sharpens the novel-state problem in 01.6, because a system that has passed through a bifurcation may have entered a regime that did not exist in the agent’s prior state space.

John Holland. Can useful representations emerge through adaptation rather than being specified in advance?

Holland’s work on adaptation and complex adaptive systems brought together adaptive agents, learning, rules, interaction and changing environments. The relevant question: can an agent learn a representation useful for prediction and action without that representation being explicitly designed? This points toward modern learned latent states and world models, where $h_t \rightarrow z_t$ produces a compressed representation useful for decision-making without necessarily reconstructing the physical state.

10. Synthesis: POMDPs and the Belief State

The nine preceding traditions each contributed a piece of the problem. What was missing was a framework that brought them together formally: one that took the Bayesian idea of belief, the Markovian idea of state, Bellman’s sequential structure, Kalman’s hidden-state estimation, and Wiener’s observation-action loop, and derived from them a principled account of optimal decision-making when the physical state is never directly available. Sondik and Smallwood provided that synthesis.

Sondik and Smallwood. How should decisions be made when the physical state itself is never directly available?

The work of Richard Smallwood and Edward Sondik in the early 1970s formalised the Partially Observable Markov Decision Process and established that an agent facing hidden state can reason over a distribution of possible states, the belief state $b_t(s) = P(s_t = s \mid h_t)$, and that this belief state is itself a sufficient statistic for optimal decision-making. The control problem becomes $b_t \rightarrow a_t$ rather than $s_t \rightarrow a_t$: the state of knowledge becomes a state for decision-making. This is the formal synthesis that connects the belief tradition, the decision tradition, the sequential-action tradition and the hidden-state control tradition into the framework that Area 01 works directly with.

A Multi-Perspective Genealogy

The history of Area 01 can be read not as a single sequence of inventions but as several questions converging on the same modern problem.

Belief. Bayes to Ramsey to de Finetti to Savage. How should uncertainty be represented and connected to action?

Decision. Wald to Blackwell to Raiffa and Schlaifer. Which information actually improves a decision, and how much is another observation worth?

Sequential action. Markov to Bellman to Howard. How should present decisions account for future consequences?

Hidden-state control. Wiener to Bateson to Shannon to Ashby to Kalman. How can a system be regulated when its relevant state is not directly observable? And what changes when the observer is part of the system being observed?

Strategic uncertainty. von Neumann and Morgenstern to Harsanyi. How should an agent act when uncertainty concerns other decision makers?

Bounded intelligence. Simon. What happens when the agent cannot compute the theoretically optimal solution?

Radical and distributed uncertainty. Knight to Hayek. What if uncertainty cannot be reduced to a complete known state space, or relevant knowledge is distributed across many agents?

Probabilistic perception. Brunswik to Gibson. What must an organism infer from imperfect cues, and what information is sufficient for action rather than reconstruction?

Adaptive systems. Bertalanffy to Prigogine to Holland. How do useful representations and behaviours emerge inside interacting, adaptive systems? And what happens when the uncertainty belongs to the dynamics rather than only to the observer?

Synthesis. Sondik and Smallwood. How should decisions be made when the physical state itself remains uncertain?

Together, these perspectives prevent Area 01 from being reduced to a single question of POMDP optimisation. They expose several fundamentally different problems:

\(\text{I do not know the state}\) \(\text{I cannot observe the state}\) \(\text{I cannot estimate the state exactly}\) \(\text{I cannot afford to obtain all information}\) \(\text{I cannot compute the exact belief}\) \(\text{the relevant knowledge is distributed}\) \(\text{the true situation may not exist in my model}\) \(\text{I may not need the full state to act correctly}\)

These are not equivalent forms of uncertainty. This gives Area 01 a broader foundational question: what form of knowledge is sufficient for action when complete knowledge is unavailable, impossible, computationally infeasible, distributed, or unnecessary?

And it sharpens the two central distinctions:

\[\boxed{\text{state uncertainty} \neq \text{decision uncertainty}}\] \[\boxed{\text{information gain} \neq \text{decision value}}\]

F-Series: Foundation Experiments

Each F-series experiment takes one foundational proposition, constructs a minimal simulation environment, and tests whether the proposition holds, and under what conditions it breaks down or reverses.

Every experiment compares the agent that implements the mechanism against a baseline without it, and against versions that combine mechanisms from different traditions. Some propositions will survive. Some will hold only in constrained regimes. Some will prove false in ways that are themselves informative.

Mimicry Sources

Natural and human systems have developed mechanisms for acting under incomplete information that formal theory has not fully absorbed. This area treats each source as a system to be analysed on its own terms, extracts the mechanism it uses, and attempts to transfer that mechanism to an autonomous agent in simulation.

Animal Foraging Under Incomplete Information. Animals must decide whether to exploit a known resource, explore another location, wait or leave. Candidate mechanisms: uncertainty-driven exploration, patch leaving, risk sensitivity, evidence accumulation.

Human Active Sensing and Gaze Control. Humans do not observe the entire visual world at equal resolution. Gaze actively selects information. Perception is itself action. Candidate mechanism: observation as a controllable, costly resource.

Statistical Decision-Making in Medicine. Medical decisions frequently involve choosing among ACT, TEST and WAIT. Additional measurements have cost, delay and potential information value. Candidate mechanism: explicit value-of-information reasoning before commitment.

Search and Rescue. Agents operate with incomplete maps, uncertain target locations, unreliable communication and changing hazards. Candidate mechanism: coordination under degraded shared information.

Scientific Experimentation. An experiment is an action partly selected because of the information it may reveal. Candidate mechanism: action selection by epistemic value rather than immediate reward.

M-Series: Mimicry Experiments

Each M-series experiment begins by characterising how a natural or human system handles a specific form of uncertainty, then attempts to transfer that mechanism to an autonomous agent in a controlled simulation environment.

The question in each case is not whether the system is optimal by formal criteria. It is whether the mechanism transfers, and what it can do that formal methods alone cannot easily replicate.

M.Gaze Human gaze control Perception as action, selective resolution Does an agent that actively selects what to observe outperform one that receives all observations passively?

State of the Art: 2024 to 2026

The contemporary problem of decision-making under uncertainty is no longer adequately described as simply solving POMDPs. The classical POMDP formulation remains the main normative framework for sequential decision-making when state is hidden. However, the research frontier increasingly concerns a broader set of questions: what representation should replace unavailable state information, how much history is necessary, whether uncertainty should be represented explicitly, when information itself should become an action, how latency and missing observations should be handled, and how uncertainty should interact with safety and risk.

Across work published between 2024 and 2026, five representations repeatedly appear as candidates:

\[o_t \qquad h_t \qquad \hat{s}_t \qquad b_t \qquad z_t\]

current observation, observation-action history, estimated state, explicit belief over hidden states, and learned latent representation. The current research problem is increasingly one of representational sufficiency: what internal representation contains enough information for the decision, without requiring the agent to reconstruct everything about the physical world?

This review is organised by research problem rather than by year. The connection to each project in this area is noted where it is direct.

1. Partial observability as a representation problem

A major direction in 2024 was to question the common practice of treating observations as Markov states. Zhang, Ren, Xiao, Schuurmans and Dai’s Provable Representation with Efficient Planning for Partially Observable Reinforcement Learning (ICML 2024) starts from the observation that conflating observations with states violates the MDP assumption under partial observability. Rather than simply adding recurrence, they develop a representation-based framework with theoretical guarantees, shifting the central question from $o_t = s_t?$ to $\phi(h_t) = ?$ where $\phi$ is a representation capable of supporting decision-making.

This aligns directly with the working distinction in Area 01 between world state and decision-relevant representation.

A contemporary robotics example of the same move appears in Ayala, Sui, Cruz, Torok, Deghat and Fernandes’ 2026 preprint Self-Predictive Representation for Autonomous UAV Object-Goal Navigation, which trains a self-predictive state representation for UAV navigation where the target location is not directly given. Their learned representation is then coupled with model-free actor-critic algorithms. The result opens a precise question for 01.3: is a learned latent representation approximating a belief state, compressing history in a different form, or learning only the distinctions necessary for control?

2. Memory: architecture, not just capacity

A straightforward response to partial observability is to retain history. Modern research increasingly shows that the relevant question is not simply whether an agent has memory but what memory architecture preserves the right information.

Lu, Shi, Liu, Hu, Du and Xu’s Rethinking Transformers in Solving POMDPs (ICML 2024) studies whether Transformers are automatically suitable for partially observable sequential decision problems. Their theoretical analysis identifies classes of POMDP structure that are difficult for standard Transformers to capture and motivates recurrent inductive biases. Empirically, their Deep Linear Recurrent Unit performed strongly in the studied settings. The implication: increasing general sequence-model capacity does not automatically solve memory under partial observability.

In 2025, Eberhard, Muehlebach and Vernade’s Partially Observable Reinforcement Learning with Memory Traces (ICML 2025) proposed using exponentially weighted memory traces as compact summaries of observation history. This creates a continuum from current observation to finite window to recurrent state to compressed trace to explicit belief. The unresolved question is no longer how much memory? but what information from history must survive compression for the decision to remain correct? That question is what Project 01.2 directly investigates.

3. Belief states: when are they necessary

Deep RL has not displaced belief-state reasoning.

Zamboni, Cirino, Restelli and Mutti’s How to Explore with Belief: State Entropy Maximization in POMDPs (ICML 2024) demonstrates why reasoning directly about observations can be misleading. Under partial observability, diversity in observation space may differ substantially from entropy over the underlying hidden states. Their method formulates exploration objectives through belief states. This is a direct empirical demonstration of the central distinction of this area:

\[\text{information visible to the agent} \neq \text{information about the hidden world}\]

Zhu and Lu’s A Covering Framework for Offline POMDPs Learning Using Belief Space Metric (AISTATS 2026) returns to belief space for a different reason: long histories create both a curse of horizon and a curse of memory, and belief states can compress many histories that are equivalent with respect to the hidden-state distribution. The current tension is therefore $h_t \rightarrow b_t$ versus $h_t \rightarrow z_t$, with classical POMDP theory giving $b_t$ a precise probabilistic interpretation while modern representation learning often produces $z_t$ without requiring that interpretation. Whether interpretability as a posterior distribution is necessary for robust decision-making remains open and is what 01.3 will examine.

The closest Australian line to this problem is Hanna Kurniawati’s group at ANU, whose programme spans planning under uncertainty, scalable POMDP solvers and robotics. Their recent work on vectorised online POMDP planning (VOPP) represents belief structures as tensors and massively parallelises online computation, reporting substantial gains over previous solvers. ANU is now explicitly exploring this solver for physical robot navigation and automatic POMDP model generation.

4. Missing, noisy, delayed: treating them separately

A particularly important development for 01.1 is that recent research increasingly refuses to collapse all imperfect observation into generic partial observability.

Chen, Meng, Bai, Ye, Poor and Wang’s Efficient Reinforcement Learning With Impaired Observability (IEEE Transactions on Information Theory, 2024) directly studies latency and lost observations as distinct phenomena, deriving regret results for both settings and comparing achievable performance under each form of impairment with a full-observation reference. This supports the experimental design of 01.1, where partial, noisy, missing and delayed observations are manipulated separately before being combined.

The distinction becomes sharper in Karamzade, Kim, Lanier, Corsi and Fox’s Model-Based Reinforcement Learning under Random Observation Delays (L4DC 2026). They consider observations that can arrive randomly and out of sequence and show that simply stacking previous observations is insufficient in their setting. Their solution explicitly updates a belief according to the timing of incoming observations and integrates this mechanism with a world model. The methodological implication for 01.1:

An observation can contain correct information and still be harmful if the agent does not know what time that information describes.

Delay therefore changes the information structure, not merely the amount of information. This is one reason why 01.1 should experimentally manipulate missingness and delay separately rather than treating them as equivalent degradations.

An additional finding relevant to 01.1 comes from Meng, Gorbet, Burke and Kulić at Monash and CSIRO (Multi-step first: A lightweight deep reinforcement learning strategy for robust continuous control with partial observability, Neural Networks 2026). They test PPO, TD3 and SAC on partially observable continuous-control tasks and report that performance rankings differ from commonly observed full-state results: PPO was more robust in their partial-observation experiments, and multi-step targets improved the other algorithms. This warns against treating the baseline algorithm as neutral. A policy class that performs best under $o_t = s_t$ may not remain best under $o_t \neq s_t$. This adds a methodological requirement to 01.1: the information-degradation curve should eventually be tested across more than one algorithm to determine whether it is algorithm-dependent.

5. Observation as action

A clear direction from 2025 onward is the transition from passive sensing to active information acquisition.

Li and Oliva’s Towards Cost Sensitive Decision Making (AISTATS 2025) introduces an Active-Acquisition POMDP in which the agent can acquire otherwise unavailable features at a cost. Their method uses generative modelling to infer missing features and hierarchical RL to jointly reason about acquisition and task decisions. The question becomes: act now, or obtain information and then act?

Gao, Xu, Rawashdeh, Klasnja and Murphy extend this in Active Measuring in Reinforcement Learning With Delayed Negative Effects (AISTATS 2026). Their formulation lets the agent explicitly choose whether to measure latent state, where measurement itself has a cost or delayed negative consequence. Their solution uses belief states and sequential Monte Carlo approximation. This is a substantial conceptual shift: the observation channel is no longer fixed as $s_t \rightarrow o_t$ but is partly controlled by the agent through $a_t^{sense} \rightarrow o_{t+1}$. This provides the contemporary foundation for Project 01.4.

In Australian robotics, Tran, Perera, Garratt, Kasmarik and Anavatti’s Active Sensing Strategy: Multi-Modal, Multi-Robot Source Localization and Mapping in Real-World Settings (Journal of Field Robotics, 2025) combines coverage-based exploration with active sensing for gas-field estimation using multiple robots in physical environments. This work, from UNSW Canberra, is the most direct Australian field demonstration of the problems 01.4 studies in simulation.

6. Partial observability and safety

Uncertainty is no longer treated in current research merely as a source of reduced reward.

Huang, Wang, Li, Xia, Zhang and Zhang’s PIGDreamer: Privileged Information Guided World Models for Safe Partially Observable Reinforcement Learning (ICML 2025) formulates a safe RL problem under partial observability and studies the use of privileged information during training to improve learned world-model representations while deployment remains partially observable.

Yao, Zhang, Xia, Wang, Roy-Chowdhury and Li’s Towards Generalizable Safety in Crowd Navigation via Conformal Uncertainty Handling (CoRL 2025) takes a different route: uncertainty estimates over pedestrian predictions are added to robot observations and used within constrained RL, with results demonstrated on a physical robot.

Together these papers argue that Area 01 should not evaluate uncertainty purely through average return. Tail behaviour, constraint violations and catastrophic-event frequency are necessary complements. That is the bridge from partial observability into Project 01.5.

7. Unknown situations and distributional shift

Classical partial observability assumes the hidden state belongs to some known space $s_t \in \mathcal{S}$. Project 01.6 asks what happens when that assumption itself fails.

Grooten et al.’s SPARC (AAAI 2026) addresses control under unseen contextual conditions without explicit access to context at test time, which is structurally the same problem. Recent offline RL surveys organise current approaches around distribution shift, OOD actions and uncertainty-aware policy restrictions, emphasising that policies trained from fixed data can behave poorly when the evaluation distribution differs from training. The problem moves from $P(s_i \mid h_t)$ to a prior question: is $s_t$ inside $\mathcal{S}$ at all? An agent may be highly confident within the wrong model class. That is qualitatively different from being uncertain between known alternatives, and it is what 01.6 directly targets.

8. The Australian landscape

Three Australian research lines are directly relevant to the Area 01 experimental programme, attacking different points in the chain from observation to unknown situations.

ANU / Kurniawati is the closest Australian line to the classical core of Area 01 through POMDP planning, belief-space decision-making and scalable online solvers. Their work is the primary Australian reference for 01.3.

Monash and CSIRO / Kulić and Burke provide the most directly comparable Australian methodology to 01.1: testing standard RL algorithms under induced partial observability rather than beginning with a specialised solver. Their finding that algorithm rankings change under partial observation is a concrete methodological warning for our experimental design.

UNSW Canberra / Kasmarik and Garratt provide the active sensing and multi-robot physical deployment line most relevant to 01.4, with additional connections to risk-aware search relevant to 01.5.

Francisco Cruz and the A2R2 group at UNSW Sydney contribute to Area 01 at specific points, the self-predictive UAV representation work for 01.3, and affordance-based state representations for the general question of decision-relevant state, but their programme is not primarily a classical POMDP line. Their strongest connections to this portfolio run through Area 02 (Interactive RL) and Area 14 (Adaptive Multi-Source Learning).

What remains unresolved

The literature does not indicate that partial observability has been solved. There is still no general answer to whether an agent should operate on $o_t$, $h_t$, $\hat{s}_t$, $b_t$ or $z_t$. Different approaches succeed under different assumptions.

The literature also lacks an empirical taxonomy telling us when memory is enough, when explicit state estimation is necessary, when belief distributions matter, and when new observations must be actively acquired. Missing observations, noise and delays remain unevenly studied, and recent results increasingly demonstrate that these perturbations should not be treated as interchangeable.

Perhaps most importantly for this portfolio, the literature usually asks how performance can be recovered under partial observability. Our first project asks a prior question: what exactly happens to decision-making as different forms of information are removed or corrupted? Before introducing memory, belief states, active sensing or risk-aware control, we want to construct the missing empirical map:

\[\boxed{\text{information degradation} \rightarrow \text{decision degradation}}\]

and determine whether that relationship is smooth, thresholded, variable-specific, algorithm-dependent or decision-dependent. That is where 01.1 begins.

Common Metrics

These metrics apply across all three series. Exact operationalisation is fixed during each experiment’s formalisation.

Return. $J(\pi) = \mathbb{E}\left[\sum_t \gamma^t r_t\right]$

Success Rate. Probability of completing the task.

Performance Retention. $PR(\theta) = J(\theta) / J_{\text{full}}$, how much nominal capability remains under information degradation, where $\theta$ parameterises the degree of degradation (noise level, dropout probability, delay, hidden fraction). $PR = 1$ means no degradation; $PR = 0$ means complete failure.

Decision Disagreement. $D(\theta) = P\left[a_t^{\text{uncertain}} \neq a_t^{\text{full}}\right]$, how frequently imperfect information actually changes the decision. This metric directly operationalises the central distinction of this area.

Information Sensitivity. $IS(\theta) = -\partial J / \partial \theta$, how rapidly performance changes as $\theta$ increases. High sensitivity indicates a fragile regime; low sensitivity indicates robustness.

Calibration. When probabilities are represented explicitly: does the agent’s stated confidence correspond to observed frequencies?

Tail Risk. Failure probability, worst-case outcome, CVaR and catastrophic-event frequency. Applied wherever consequences are asymmetric.

Phenomena We Want to Discover

The experiments should search for structure, not merely algorithm rankings.

Information Thresholds. Does performance decline gradually, or is there a critical value of $\theta$ after which behaviour collapses?

Critical Variables. Can one hidden variable matter more than many visible variables?

Recoverable Uncertainty. Can history reconstruct missing information?

Irrecoverable Uncertainty. Are some states observationally indistinguishable regardless of memory?

Decision-Equivalent Uncertainty. Can uncertainty remain high while the correct action remains unchanged?

Dangerous Confidence. Can the agent become confidently wrong?

Value of Another Observation. When does one additional observation actually change the decision?

Autonomy Boundary. At what point should uncertainty change $ACT$ into $LOOK$, $WAIT$, $ASK$, $YIELD$ or $ABSTAIN$?

Active Questions

On observation. How much does partial observation degrade decision quality? Which variables are actually decision-relevant? How do noise, missingness, ambiguity and delay differ in their effects? Which combinations are particularly damaging?

On memory. When can the past reconstruct the present? How much history is sufficient? Can too much history become computationally wasteful or harmful? What should memory preserve?

On belief. When is explicit uncertainty representation necessary? Is a point estimate sufficient? Can learned latent states substitute for explicit beliefs?

On information seeking. When should the agent observe before acting? Should it maximise information gain or expected decision improvement? When should it stop sensing?

On risk. How should uncertainty interact with asymmetric consequences? When should rare events dominate decision-making?

On unknown situations. Can the agent distinguish ignorance from novelty? Can it recognise when its own representation is inadequate? When should it abstain?

Unresolved Questions

What is sufficient information? Can decision-relevant sufficiency be characterised without reconstructing the complete physical state?

Is belief the right primitive? Classical POMDP theory privileges $b_t$. Modern agents increasingly use learned latent states $z_t$. Are these different implementations of the same principle or fundamentally different representations?

Is uncertainty reduction always useful? An observation could greatly reduce entropy while leaving the preferred action unchanged. Reducing uncertainty may not matter if the additional information cannot change what the agent does.

What is the relationship between memory and belief? Does memory merely store evidence from which belief can be inferred? Can a recurrent policy behave optimally without representing anything interpretable as belief?

When is uncertainty irreducible? Some hidden information may be inferable from history. Some may require active sensing. Some may be fundamentally unavailable. These are not the same problem.

When should an autonomous system refuse to decide? Traditional control asks which action to take. A more general autonomy problem may require choosing among ${ACT, LOOK, WAIT, ASK, YIELD, ABSTAIN}$. What determines which option is appropriate?

The Deeper Question

Decision-Making Under Uncertainty initially appears to be a technical problem involving noisy sensors, POMDPs and reinforcement learning algorithms.

But underneath those methods are three distinct questions that should not be collapsed:

\[\boxed{\text{What do I observe?}}\] \[\boxed{\text{What should I believe?}}\] \[\boxed{\text{What should I do?}}\]

Better observation does not automatically produce better belief about what matters for the decision. Better belief does not necessarily change the decision. More information is not necessarily more useful information. Lower uncertainty does not automatically produce safer behaviour. And sometimes the intelligent action may be to obtain information rather than to act on the world.

The working proposition for Area 01:

Intelligence under uncertainty may depend not on eliminating uncertainty, but on identifying which uncertainty matters for action and responding appropriately to it.

This remains a proposition. The F-series tests the foundations. The M-series tests what natural systems know that formal theory has not yet absorbed. The research projects test what survives when both are placed under controlled experimental pressure.
