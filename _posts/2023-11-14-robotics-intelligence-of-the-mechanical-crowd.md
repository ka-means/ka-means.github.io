When you watch a flock of starlings murmurating at dusk, it is hard not to look for the invisible conductor. There must be someone, somewhere, calling the tune. But there is not. The breathtaking coordination we see is the result of each bird paying attention to just its closest neighbors, adjusting its speed and direction based on local information. In modern robotics, we are attempting a similar feat with swarms of autonomous systems.

The core challenge is not simply programming a single machine to move. The real problem is designing the network topology. Think of this topology as the invisible map that dictates who can talk to whom. If a robot in the swarm loses its signal, gets stuck, or breaks down, how does the rest of the group know what to do without bringing the entire mission to a grinding halt?

Instead of relying on a central brain to dictate every move, we are trying to design structural coordination capacities. We want each machine to make local decisions based on its limited perspective, trusting that these local interactions will result in a surprisingly intelligent global behavior.
