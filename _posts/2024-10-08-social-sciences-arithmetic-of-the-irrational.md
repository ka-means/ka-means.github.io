Many of us grew up with the idea that human beings are strictly rational actors. The assumption was that, faced with a choice, our brains perform a rapid cost-benefit analysis and select the option that maximizes our utility.

Behavioral economics consistently proves this idea is a fiction. We value an object more simply because we already own it, and we make objectively worse financial decisions when we are tired or hungry. Mathematically, these behaviors shatter the expected utility theory, introducing variables of context and emotion that simply do not fit into a clean spreadsheet.

Our decisions are deeply molded by the architecture of our environment and our inherent cognitive limits.

## Questions that arise from thinking about this topic

- If our choices are truly so malleable and vulnerable to the design of the world around us, why do we continue to design institutions, laws, and educational systems that assume citizens are perfect, emotionless calculators?
